Who should the bank call first?

Presented by Team 4BC6141 Machine Learning

A Portuguese bank can call only one in five clients during a term-deposit campaign. We trained ten models to rank the call list and tested whether they beat the rule the call centre uses today.

41,176client contact records, May 2008 to Nov 2010
11.3%of them subscribed
20%of the list can be called
10models, built by 5 teammates on identical data
Decision
Before a calling wave, rank clients so the top 20% of calls reach as many subscribers as possible.
Today's rule
Call clients whose previous campaign ended in "success" first, then "failure", then everyone else.
Our question
Does a model built only on information known before the call rank clients better than that rule?
Scorecard
Average precision (how clean the top of the list is) and subscribers reached per 100 calls at 20% capacity.

The world changed during the data

Subscription rate across the records in date order. It jumps from about 5% to over 30% in the final months, a warning we return to in the drift check.

What we left out on purpose

Call duration, number of calls this campaign, month and weekday of the last call are only known during or after calling. Using them would be cheating: a model with call duration scores about 0.59 instead of 0.44, but it could never be used to decide whom to call.

The rulebook every notebook followed

  • Load the same frozen files and check their fingerprints.
  • Fill gaps, scale and encode inside each fold, never on all the data at once.
  • Try each model's settings and pick the one with the best validation score.
  • Score the test set once, after choosing. No changes afterwards.
  • Save the scores in one standard format so they can be combined.

Three scores, three questions

Training score: how well does it describe the clients it learned from?

Validation score: how well does it rank clients it had not seen? This is how settings were chosen.

Test score: the one-time final exam on locked clients.

If training is much higher than validation, the model memorised (overfitting). If both are low, it was too simple (underfitting).

Reading a call list

Average precision (AP) summarises how clean the top of the list is: 0.113 means random, 1.0 means perfect.

Step 6 · Drift check

Trained on the past, tested on the future, the advantage shrinks

We refitted the three family winners on the earliest 80% of records and tested them on the latest 20%, with no retuning. Subscription in that later period was 30.8%, against 6.4% earlier.

Lift at 20% capacity: random split vs future period

What this means

On a later period, the random forest still beats today's rule, but only narrowly (lift 1.94 vs 1.83). KNN and Elastic Net fall below it. The rule hardly moves, because "succeeded last time" means the same thing in any year.

Lift is used here because the share of subscribers differs between the two periods, which changes what AP can reach.

Step 7 · Verdict

Rank with a random forest, then pilot it against the rule

  1. Rank clients with the random forest and call the top 20% first. Gradient boosting and bagging performed equally well.
  2. On test clients, that reached 36 subscribers per 100 calls versus 21 with today's rule: about 15 more per 100 calls.
  3. The same calls would reach 64% of all subscribers instead of 38%.
  4. Conditions shifted sharply over time. On a later period the advantage shrank to lift 1.94 vs 1.83, so pilot the model against the rule and retrain it on recent campaigns.
  5. The model shows who is likely to subscribe, not why. Economic indicators and past outcomes are associations, not levers.

What we learned as a team

Any model nearly doubled today's rule. Which model mattered much less: ten models span only 0.43 to 0.46.

Five linear models scored within 0.002 of each other. With 33,000 rows, the type of penalty made no practical difference.

Honest testing mattered most: removing call duration and checking a later period changed the story more than any model choice.
